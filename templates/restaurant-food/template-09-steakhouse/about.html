Our House

The dry-age room runs the show

Walk into Emberline and the first thing you meet is the beef. Behind the host stand, a glass-walled room glows cold, racks of ribeye and sirloin resting on the bone at two degrees, some of them for forty days or more.

That room is not decoration; it is the kitchen’s slowest, most important station. Head grillardin Marcus Bell treats dry-aging as the real cooking — weeks of patience that concentrate the flavour long before a single cut hits the fire.

When the time comes, the cooking is fast and loud. Cuts are seared hard over a roaring grill, rested long, and sliced at the pass so you can hear it. Fire fast, rest long, slice loud — that’s the whole philosophy on a plate.

The room is dark walnut and ember light, built for long dinners and lower voices. We pour big reds, we carve for two, and we are unapologetic about the fact that the centre of the plate is a very good piece of beef.

Everything around the steak earns its place — the bone-marrow butter, the hand-cut chips, the cellar of reds — but nothing distracts from it. At Emberline, the age room sets the standard and the grill just keeps up.

How we cook

Dry-aged on the bone

Seared over fire

When it’s time, the grill runs screaming hot for a hard, dark crust, then a long rest before it’s carved at the pass.

A cellar of big reds

The list leans into bold, age-worthy bottles built to stand up to dry-aged beef. The sommelier will steer you true.
