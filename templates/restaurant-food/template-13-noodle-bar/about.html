Our Story

Four generations of dough slap

At Tangle, the noodles are pulled by hand at the counter, and the rhythm in Wei Lin’s arms is older than the restaurant by about a century. He learned lamian from his grandmother, who learned it from hers, in a line that stretches back four generations.

The whole bar is built around that one skill. A single piece of dough is stretched, folded and slapped against the bench again and again until it splits into dozens of strands — no machine, no cutter, just timing, gluten and an unhurried wrist.

You can’t rush dough; you can only keep it company. The flour rests, the broth simmers for hours, and the noodles are pulled to order so they hit the bowl seconds after they’re born. That’s why the texture is springy and alive rather than slick.

The broths are the quiet half of the work — bones and aromatics simmered low overnight until they’re deep and clear. Chili oil is made in house, the toppings are prepped fresh, and the bowls are assembled fast so nothing sits.

It’s a counter-seat, slurp-loud, walk-in kind of place. Watch the pulling, listen for your number, and eat the moment the bowl lands — a hand-pulled noodle waits for no one, least of all politely.
