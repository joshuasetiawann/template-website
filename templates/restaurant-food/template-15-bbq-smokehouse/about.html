Pit Story

The kettle that started the fire

Black Kettle is named for the grill that started everything: a dented, second-hand kettle that pitmaster Otis Crane kept burning behind a tire shop, learning brisket the long way — one overnight cook, one mistake, one fix at a time.

Years of those overnights taught him the only lesson that matters: the fire doesn’t care about your schedule. Brisket is done when it’s done, not when the clock says so, and trying to hurry it is how you ruin good meat.

Today the kettle has grown into a row of offset smokers out back, but the method hasn’t changed. Post oak and patience, low heat and long hours, salt-and-pepper bark and nothing to hide behind. Everything is smoked daily and sold till it’s gone.

When the day’s brisket, ribs and pork run out, that’s it — we don’t hold meat back or stretch it thin. The board comes down, the pit cools, and tomorrow’s cook starts in the dark. It’s the honest way to run a smokehouse, even if it means selling out by two.

The room is smoke-dark and ember-warm, the trays are lined with butcher paper, and the line forms early on a good day. Grab a number, follow the smoke, and don’t come at four expecting the brisket to still be there.

How we smoke

Smoked daily, sold out

We smoke fresh every morning and sell till it’s gone. Nothing is held back or stretched — when the board comes down, that’s it.

Bark, not sauce

Salt-and-pepper bark and clean smoke do the work. Sauce is on the side, not a disguise — the meat stands on its own.
