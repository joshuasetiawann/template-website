The Itamae

Twenty years for ten seats

Kanade has ten seats and one chef at its centre. That is not a constraint to be solved — it is the entire idea. Sato Kenji wanted a counter small enough to cook for everyone himself, and patient enough to do it properly.

He trained for the better part of two decades, first in Kanazawa and then in Tokyo, where he learned that the rice is the cooking and the fish is merely the weather. Shari — seasoned, body-temperature rice — is judged here before anything else.

The menu is omakase: you sit, and the evening is handed to you piece by piece, paced to the room. What appears depends on the morning market and the season; the chef decides the order, the timing and when to stop.

The room is deliberately spare. An unmarked door, a cypress counter, a single line of light. Nothing competes with the food, and conversation tends to soften to match. We ask guests to arrive a few minutes early so the first piece lands at its best.

Kanade means a harmony or a melody, and that is how an omakase is built — not a list of dishes but a sequence, each piece tuned to the one before it, rising and resolving across the meal.

Shari, judged first

The rice is seasoned, warm and pressed by hand to the exact density of the fish it carries. Get the rice wrong and nothing else matters.

Market every morning

We buy what the day and the season offer, then build the omakase around it. The fish chooses the menu, not the other way around.
