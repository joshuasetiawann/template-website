Our story

Where the trail ends and the fire begins

Blackpine Hollow has stood at the head of the valley since 1968, built by a family of guides who wanted a roof at the end of the longest trail. They hauled the timber up by mule and quarried the stone from the ridge above; the great fireplace in the hall has not gone cold in a winter since.

We are deliberately hard to reach. The last few miles are four-wheel-drive only, there is no through-road, and the nearest town is an hour of switchbacks below. What you trade in convenience you gain in silence — the kind that only arrives when the engine stops and the valley closes in behind you.

The lodge runs on wood, water and good sense. A hydro turbine on the creek carries the lights, the stoves burn fallen timber from our own slopes, and the kitchen leans on what keeps through a mountain winter: cured meat, root vegetables, bread baked at altitude and stew that simmers all day.

Most of all we are a base camp. The guides who built the place still keep the routes, and the boot room is the busiest part of the lodge at dawn. Whether you are walking out for an afternoon or roping up for the high ridge, the fire and the stew will be waiting when you get back.

Sustainability

Off-grid by necessity, by choice

There is no mains power at 2,140 metres, so the lodge makes its own. A small hydro turbine on the creek runs the lights and the kitchen, wood from our slopes heats the rooms, and what little waste we cannot compost goes back down the mountain with us. Living lightly up here is simply how the place survives.
