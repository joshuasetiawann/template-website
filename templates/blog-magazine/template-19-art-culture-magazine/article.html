In the Studio With the Sculptor Who Casts in Harbor Mud

Petra Ilves digs her material at low tide and lets the river finish every piece. The kiln schedule obeys the moon.

LA

Lou Andrade

Jun 4, 2026·9 min read

The room went quiet in the particular way rooms do when something is actually new. Petra Ilves digs her material at low tide and lets the river finish every piece. The kiln schedule obeys the moon.

What follows is less a verdict than a working argument, assembled from notes, false starts and one or two opinions I have since revised. The headline is up top; everything below it has to earn the claim or quietly take it back.

What the work is asking of us

The room went quiet in the particular way rooms do when something is actually new — not louder, not more spectacular, but more demanding. The work does not perform for you; it waits, and asks whether you are willing to meet it halfway.

Criticism loves a verdict, but the interesting art resists the thumbs-up. This piece is not 'good' or 'bad' in any way that helps; it is a question made physical, and the only honest review is a record of what it did to the person standing in front of it.

What lingers is not the spectacle but the argument it starts on the walk home. The best cultural work is a delayed reaction — it seems modest in the room and then refuses to leave your head for a week, rearranging things quietly.

What it left me thinking about

  • The gap between what the work shows and what it withholds, which is where the meaning lives.
  • A use of space and silence that does more than any wall text could explain.
  • The uncomfortable sense of being implicated rather than merely addressed.

Where the conversation goes next

Be patient with the boring middle. Beginnings are theatrical and endings are quotable, but the middle is where the real decisions live, unglamorous and undocumented. The block below is the part I would clip and keep.

Three things to carry out the door

  • Start smaller than feels respectable; momentum is the only currency that compounds.
  • Write down the thing you are afraid to be wrong about. Revisit it in a month.
  • Borrow ruthlessly, attribute generously, and keep the version that survives contact with reality.

Go and stand in front of it yourself, because no description survives the encounter. The point of work like this is not to be summarised but to be met, and the meeting is the part that no review can do for you.
