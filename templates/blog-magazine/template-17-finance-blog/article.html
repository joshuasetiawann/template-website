What follows is less a verdict than a working argument, assembled from notes, false starts and one or two opinions I have since revised. The headline is up top; everything below it has to earn the claim or quietly take it back.

Why the boring answer wins

The boring answer is usually correct, which is precisely why it gets ignored. Low fees, broad diversification, a holding period measured in decades and the discipline to do nothing in a panic — it is unglamorous, unsexy, and quietly devastating to the competition.

Most of the cost of investing is not the market; it is us. The fees we do not notice, the trades we cannot resist, the tax we trigger chasing a story. The single highest-return skill in personal finance is the ability to sit still while the noise insists you act.

None of this is advice for your specific situation, and the numbers below are illustrative, not a promise. But the principle generalises: control the things you can — cost, behaviour, time in the market — and make peace with the things you cannot.

What actually moves the needle

  • Fees, because a percent a year compounds into a decade of retirement you will miss.
  • Behaviour, because the gap between investor returns and fund returns is mostly self-inflicted.
  • Time, the one input that costs nothing and outperforms every clever timing strategy on average.
