Stairwells of the Eastern Bloc: A Vertical Typology

Forty-one stairwells, one lens, zero people. The geometry does the talking in this season's most disciplined series.

EC

Emil Castaneda

Jun 4, 2026·6 min read

The frame was already there; I just had to stop walking long enough to notice. Forty-one stairwells, one lens, zero people. The geometry does the talking in this season's most disciplined series.

What follows is less a verdict than a working argument, assembled from notes, false starts and one or two opinions I have since revised. The headline is up top; everything below it has to earn the claim or quietly take it back.

What the frame was waiting for

The picture was already there; the only craft was stopping long enough to notice it. Photography, at least the kind I care about, is less about the gear and more about the willingness to be patient and slightly conspicuous in public.

Light does the heavy lifting and asks for nothing but attention. The same street is three different photographs across a single morning, and the difference between a snapshot and an image is usually just the discipline to wait for the better one.

I have stopped chasing the dramatic shot. The quiet frame — an ordinary moment caught at the precise second it stops being ordinary — outlasts the spectacular one every time. Spectacle impresses; specificity endures.

What I look for before I raise the camera

  • The edge of the light, where ordinary things briefly become worth photographing.
  • A gesture about to happen, which means watching people rather than the viewfinder.
  • What to leave out, because a photograph is mostly an argument about the frame's edges.

How to make your own

Be patient with the boring middle. Beginnings are theatrical and endings are quotable, but the middle is where the real decisions live, unglamorous and undocumented. The block below is the part I would clip and keep.

Three things to carry out the door

  • Start smaller than feels respectable; momentum is the only currency that compounds.
  • Write down the thing you are afraid to be wrong about. Revisit it in a month.
  • Borrow ruthlessly, attribute generously, and keep the version that survives contact with reality.

Carry the camera, walk slower, and wait for the light to do the work. The best photograph of your day is almost always the one you had to stand still long enough to deserve.
