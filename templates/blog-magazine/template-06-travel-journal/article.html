The Night Train to Trieste Is Slower and Better

Eleven hours, two borders, one dining car with exactly four things on the menu. The sleeper berth as a philosophy.

TM

Theo Marchetti

Jun 4, 2026·9 min read

The map said two hours. The map had not met this particular stretch of road. Eleven hours, two borders, one dining car with exactly four things on the menu. The sleeper berth as a philosophy.

What follows is less a verdict than a working argument, assembled from notes, false starts and one or two opinions I have since revised. The headline is up top; everything below it has to earn the claim or quietly take it back.

Where the road stopped cooperating

The map promised two hours. The map had not accounted for a goat with strong opinions about right-of-way, a bridge that existed mainly in theory, and a viewpoint so good it cost us forty minutes we will never regret.

Travel writing loves the epiphany on the mountaintop. The truth lives in the logistics: the bus you missed, the kindness of the stranger who fed you anyway, the small humiliations that turn into the only stories worth telling at home.

I have stopped trying to 'see everything'. A place gives itself to you in one good afternoon spent properly or not at all in a week spent rushing. The itinerary is a suggestion; the detour is the trip.

What earned its place in the pack

  • A paper map for when the signal dies, which it will, usually at the worst junction.
  • A few words of the language — enough to apologise, to thank, and to order the second coffee.
  • Far fewer plans than felt responsible, leaving room for the day to surprise me.

What I would do differently

Be patient with the boring middle. Beginnings are theatrical and endings are quotable, but the middle is where the real decisions live, unglamorous and undocumented. The block below is the part I would clip and keep.

Three things to carry out the door

  • Start smaller than feels respectable; momentum is the only currency that compounds.
  • Write down the thing you are afraid to be wrong about. Revisit it in a month.
  • Borrow ruthlessly, attribute generously, and keep the version that survives contact with reality.

Go with a loose plan and a full water bottle. The best stretch of any road is almost never the one you wrote down before you left.
