'The Cartographer's Dog' Is the Year's Saddest Comedy

A border-drawing bureaucrat, a stray that keeps redrawing them. Dries Kalman's deadpan masterpiece earns every tear.

RP

Roman Petrov

Jun 4, 2026·8 min read

There is a version of this film that plays it safe, and mercifully, nobody made it. A border-drawing bureaucrat, a stray that keeps redrawing them. Dries Kalman's deadpan masterpiece earns every tear.

What follows is less a verdict than a working argument, assembled from notes, false starts and one or two opinions I have since revised. The headline is up top; everything below it has to earn the claim or quietly take it back.

What the film is really doing

Beneath the plot, this is a film about silence — about who is allowed to keep it and who is forced to fill it. The director treats a held shot the way a great novelist treats a paragraph break, and trusts you to lean in.

The central performance is the engine. It would be easy to play this character loud; the choice to play it quiet, to let the camera find the flicker rather than the speech, is what lifts the whole thing above its genre.

Does it earn its runtime? Mostly. There is a middle act that mistakes patience for pace, a subplot that deserved either more room or the scissors. But the ending lands with such precision that you forgive the detour on the way to it.

What stays with you after the credits

  • A closing image that recontextualises the two hours before it without a word of dialogue.
  • Sound design that does more characterisation than most scripts manage in a monologue.
  • A refusal to explain itself, which will frustrate some and haunt the rest.

Where it stumbles, and why it matters less than you think

Be patient with the boring middle. Beginnings are theatrical and endings are quotable, but the middle is where the real decisions live, unglamorous and undocumented. The block below is the part I would clip and keep.

The verdict

4/5

What lands

  • A central performance that earns every minute of its runtime
  • Cinematography that treats silence as a character
  • An ending that trusts the audience to do the arithmetic

What drags

  • A middle act that mistakes patience for pacing
  • One subplot that deserved either more room or the cutting-room floor

See it in a room where nobody is checking a phone. This is a film that rewards the kind of attention it is increasingly unfashionable to give, and punishes the distracted with a shrug.
