Our story

How this masthead came to be

Stackline started as an internal wiki page titled 'benchmarks people actually trust'. It collected the numbers a small platform team kept re-deriving because every vendor blog post buried the methodology, or skipped it entirely. When the page hit forty thousand words, it became obvious it wanted to be a publication.

The premise has not changed since: no claim ships without a methodology section, and no benchmark runs that we cannot reproduce on the office mini-rack. If a result only holds on a sponsor's hardware, it is marketing, and marketing goes in a different folder.

We write long because the interesting part of an engineering decision is almost never the headline number — it is the constraint that produced it, the profile that explained it, and the trade you made with your future on-call self. Every Stackline piece is reviewed by an engineer who did not write it, with a profiler open.

We are deliberately small, deliberately slow, and deliberately allergic to hype cycles. The newsletter goes out once a week, the source data is linked, and you are welcome to grep our privacy policy.

Editorial mission

To publish reproducible, vendor-neutral engineering writing — benchmarked, profiled, peer-reviewed, and honest about its constraints.

How we work

Three rules we don't bend

The standards behind everything we publish.

01

Reproduce or retract

Every benchmark runs on hardware we own and publish. If you can't repeat it from the methodology section, it isn't a result.

02

Peer review

No piece ships without an engineer who didn't write it reading it with a profiler open and a list of objections.

03

Link the data

Raw numbers, configs and scripts are linked from every analysis. Trust is a function of transparency, not tone.
