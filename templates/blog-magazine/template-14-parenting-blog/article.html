What Our Pediatric Sleep Consult Actually Cost and Taught

Three sessions, one spreadsheet of wake windows, and the permission slip we apparently needed: it's allowed to be easier.

HO

Hannah Okoye

Jun 4, 2026·9 min read

Nobody tells you that the hardest part happens after everyone goes home. Three sessions, one spreadsheet of wake windows, and the permission slip we apparently needed: it's allowed to be easier.

What follows is less a verdict than a working argument, assembled from notes, false starts and one or two opinions I have since revised. The headline is up top; everything below it has to earn the claim or quietly take it back.

The part nobody warns you about

Nobody tells you the hardest part happens after everyone goes home — in the quiet, with the advice columns contradicting each other and a small person who has not read any of them. The confidence in the parenting books is, frankly, suspicious.

What helped was lowering the bar to something a tired human could actually clear. Not perfect, not optimised, just present and roughly consistent. Kids do not need a flawless parent; they need a reliable one who occasionally apologises and means it.

I have made peace with getting it wrong daily and right often enough. The goal was never a curated childhood. It was a safe one, warm enough that the inevitable mistakes land on a soft floor instead of a hard one.

What I would tell a new parent

  • Lower the bar to 'safe and loved'; the rest is decoration you can add later.
  • Pick the two things that matter to you and let the other eight slide without guilt.
  • Apologise when you snap. It teaches more about repair than any lecture about behaviour.

What actually helped

Be patient with the boring middle. Beginnings are theatrical and endings are quotable, but the middle is where the real decisions live, unglamorous and undocumented. The block below is the part I would clip and keep.

Three things to carry out the door

  • Start smaller than feels respectable; momentum is the only currency that compounds.
  • Write down the thing you are afraid to be wrong about. Revisit it in a month.
  • Borrow ruthlessly, attribute generously, and keep the version that survives contact with reality.

You will get it wrong today and right tomorrow, and the ledger still comes out fine. Lower the bar, hold the line on the two things that matter, and forgive yourself the other eight.
